# Competitor research

## Method

In September 2026 I walked five apps on an iPhone and read every screen I captured, 561 in all. Each app got a per-screen inventory. Anything I couldn't see on a device came from official websites, help centres and store listings read the same week, and is marked as such.

| Peer | What it is | Screens read |
|---|---|---:|
| A | A mature UAE platform, regulated in two markets, the app the region is measured against | 359 |
| B | Saudi app in the Real Estate General Authority's sandbox | 83 |
| C | Saudi sandbox app | 60 |
| D | Saudi sandbox app | 51 |
| E | Saudi sandbox app, the only other usufruct product (onboarding only) | 8 |

The apps are anonymised because several captures came from logged-in accounts and record problems with those accounts.

**Coverage limits.** No app was captured through payment. Peer A reached the cart and stopped, because the account was at two of four verification steps. Of the Saudi peers, one account was unapproved, one app had no open deal, and one had no investable inventory and a blocked wallet top-up. Peer E's capture is onboarding only. So amount entry, order summaries, consent wording, payment, the success screen and a funded portfolio are unverified for all five.

## The headline

The original app's advantage was honesty about money, and it held up against every app I read, including Peer A. It was also narrower than it first looked, and it lived on one screen.

Peer A forces the claim to be stated precisely. Its deal page itemises every entry cost line, each with its own explanation sheet, and names its own fee in the list. What it doesn't put on any screen is the recurring cost: the annual administration charge, the yearly cost of the ownership vehicle and the running compliance fee appear only inside a downloadable five-year spreadsheet, where they bring the yield from roughly 7.1 to 7.5% gross down to 4.8 to 5.4% net. No exit fee appears in its interface.

So the accurate version is that the original app was the only one of the six that put every recurring percentage on screen. Peer A matched it on entry costs and beat it on explanation. Nobody matched it on what an investor pays every year and on the way out.

Everything else in the study said that the original app was competitive on substance and behind on the screens that carry it.

## What Peer A does that's worth copying

- **A track record as a product surface.** Funded and sold properties stay browsable with the whole story: exit price, gain, purchase price, funding date and cumulative rent. A new platform can't fill this on day one, but the shape should exist from day one and fill as the first deal's real numbers arrive.
- **A two-way exit waterfall.** On a sold property, cost expands into its parts, and the expected exit expands into growth, unspent budget returned and exit costs, next to the price it actually sold for. The original thesis stays on the page to be read against the outcome.
- **Correct return labels.** It names the horizon, states the annual rate separately, and puts net beside gross.
- **The underwriting in your hands.** A projections spreadsheet and a valuation report are downloadable before investing, and sold properties keep a dated valuation trail.
- **Automation as the retention story.** A monthly auto-invest plan with presets, named models and a day-of-month picker, which can be set up before verification is finished. Rent reinvestment into the highest-yielding property. Both attack the dead time between deals.
- **Loyalty tiers that unlock features.** The tiers unlock reinvestment, early access and a relationship manager as well as cashback, and locked benefits stay visible. For a platform whose constraint is supply, the equivalent is waitlist position and published allocation rules.
- **A referral offer that states its condition.** The friend is credited when approved, and the referrer only after the friend invests a minimum and the property fully funds.
- **Alerts as subscriptions.** Several named alerts, each with ranges on return, yield and price, delivered by push or email.
- **Regulatory facts in the UI.** The annual retail investment cap is a live meter with its reset date, and withholding tax for non-residents sits next to the return it reduces.
- **Exit statistics after every window,** including how many buyers there were per seller.

Where Peer A is weaker: no guest browsing, no phone or national-ID sign-up path, no recurring fee percentage in the interface, and no IRR, income calendar or payout history in the portfolio. Its help centre is English only, with no published hours.

## What didn't hold up on device

Several website claims failed on a device: 24/7 in-app chat, WhatsApp support, quarterly reports and Apple Pay at two of the Saudi peers. Across the three Saudi peers I could read in depth there was no investor count, no track record, no statements, no exports, no auto-invest, no reinvestment, no inheritance answer and no owners' voting.

Specific defects worth avoiding:

- A card and its detail page showing different yields for the same deal.
- A placeholder chart claiming 8% growth over an empty portfolio.
- "Monthly income" promised in onboarding and "annual distributions" on the card.
- A growth slide whose chart declines under a returns promise.
- The same security code called two different names in one flow.
- A lock screen that prints the account holder's full name.
- A three-year tab that shows cumulative figures under an annual label.

## Where the original app led

- **Named ongoing fees.** Annual platform fee, property management plus VAT, the exit fee, and the entry fee inside the share price, all as percentages.
- **Yields labelled net after fees,** with gross beside them.
- **Tenant, annual rent and lease end on the page.**
- **Two independent valuations,** one commissioned by each side, compared in the app.
- **Post-investment substance:** a deed tracker, the deed itself, and payout statements with the per-share arithmetic.
- **Progressive verification.** Nafath only at the point of investing. Three of the Saudi peers ask for it at sign-up. The original app was the outlier, and the outlier was right.
- **Sharia governance:** numbered decisions, an independent committee and quarterly audits.
- **Also unmatched:** the refund rule if funding fails, a referral with a stated amount, licence numbers on screen, and the usufruct product.
