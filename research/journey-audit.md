# Journey audit: fifteen stages, before and after

This is the stage-by-stage audit behind the prototype. "Before" describes the live app I audited, which isn't named here. "After" describes the Lebna prototype. All figures use Lebna's sample data, so where the original app showed a number, the equivalent sample number is used instead. The five apps I compared it with are anonymised as Peers A to E: Peer A is a mature UAE platform, and Peers B to E are Saudi apps in the Real Estate General Authority's sandbox.

The original app was read screen by screen on an iPhone (63 screens), against its four source documents: the opportunity file, two valuation reports and the subscription contract.

## 1. Arrival

What a stranger sees in the first ten seconds, and what they have to give up to see more.

**Before.** Four slides of promises, then a phone field. A visitor had to hand over a mobile number before seeing a single figure: no price, yield or property. The slides made claims that the app then refused to back up until the visitor identified themselves.

**After.** The app opens on the catalogue. Above it sits one screen of proposition (the deed in your name, rent paid monthly, shares you can sell) and one button. The phone number is asked for when someone invests, deposits or sets an alert, never at launch, and the sign-in screen names what it is unlocking.

**In the field.** Peer B shows two live deals with their minimum ticket and yield before any sign-up, and offers "enter as a guest" beside register and log in. Peer D shows what not to copy: an onboarding screen advertising a 75% annual rental yield and 5,000 properties, on a platform with one funded deal.

## 2. The gate

**Before.** A ring reading "1 of 4" sat between the visitor and the opportunities: phone, email, Nafath, customer information. Nothing had been shown yet that was worth completing four identity steps for.

**After.** Browsing is free and complete. Nafath is requested at the first investment, where motivation is highest, and the screen says why: "sign in to complete your investment". For a signed-in customer the four steps become a compact progress meter with the remaining step named.

**In the field.** Peer E asks for a phone number only, like the original app, but gets three small things right for free. It offers biometrics on the login screen, puts the country code at the start of a left-to-right field, and lets the user switch language on the very first screen.

## 3. The list

**Before.** One card, marked 100% funded, carrying a headline "total profit" figure. A first-time visitor couldn't tell what an opportunity on this platform generally looks like, what a usufruct deal is, or what happens to a deal after funding, because there was only ever one and it was finished.

**After.** A catalogue of nine records covering both products and all seven stages: open, closing, upcoming, in preparation, transferring, earning, exited, and one that failed to fund and refunded. Every card carries the same row of figures and the minimum ticket in riyals. Adding a real opportunity is a data entry, not a redesign.

**In the field.** Peer B's list card shows rental yield, capital growth and total return on three lines, with the minimum ticket underneath, so a reader can compare deals without opening one.

## 4. Between opportunities

The state the app is in most of the year.

**Before.** "We are preparing the next opportunity" over an illustration, and one button that sends a notification. Between deals the app had nothing for a user to do and nothing to capture. Demand arrived, found a closed shop and left.

**After.** The empty state does work. A visitor can join the waitlist with the amount they intend to invest, or set an alert on net yield, minimum, city and product type. Under it, the app lists the four routes by which the next opportunity reaches a user, and publishes the allocation rule in advance: subscriptions in order of arrival, no per-investor cap.

**In the field.** Peer B has zero deals available or upcoming and the screen still converts, with "add me to the waitlist" and the sandbox badge holding trust while there is nothing to buy. Peer D's empty state is the single word "soon", with no explanation, waitlist or alert.

## 5. The opportunity page

**Before.** The first three screens of scroll were a project timeline: dates that had already passed, for a deal that had already funded. The share price, the minimum, the yield and the fees were all below it. The page answered "what happened here?" before "should I buy this?"

**After.** Status and product chips, the three defined return figures, share price and minimum ticket, funding progress and time left, then property and lease, valuations, fees, documents and risks. A sticky bar carries the minimum and "invest now" the whole way down. The timeline stays, as four steps about what happens after you subscribe.

**In the field.** Peer C puts seven facts in a grid above the fold, then a 360° tour and a video, then an AI question box. Nothing is more than one screen from the top.

## 6. The numbers

**Before.** Four numbers, none defined. The headline was the annual total return multiplied by the three-year term (33.12% in Lebna's numbers), printed with no period beside the annual 11.04% it was calculated from. That figure appears in none of the four source documents. Nothing said whether 11.04% was before or after fees, what the 4.8% growth assumption was doing inside it, or where any of the numbers came from. The number a buyer needs most, the net yield after every fee, wasn't on the screen. The same card carried the advertising licence number, which makes it a licensed promotion.

**After.** Net rental yield after every fee (6.62%) with the gross yield (7.92%) printed under it. Total expected annual return (11.04%) with the 4.8% growth assumption disclosed in the same line. IRR (8.61%) with the 2% exit fee inside it. A one, three and five-year selector produces cumulative figures on demand, each labelled cumulative and carrying a note that they are in-app arithmetic, not document figures. Every number opens a sheet with its definition, its formula in words, and the document it comes from.

**In the field.** Peer B's horizon tabs repeat the original app's error: on the three-year tab, the same three labels show cumulative figures while still calling one of them annual. Peer A does it correctly. It names the horizon ("expected value of the investment over 5 years"), states the annual rate separately, and models two strategies side by side with their totals and annual rates.

## 7. The fees

**Before.** The app called the platform's fee (196,000 riyals in Lebna's numbers) a registry and brokerage charge, while the subscription contract calls the same amount the platform fee. Naming the platform's own revenue after the registry understates what it is and contradicts the document the investor signs. The list stopped there. The 1% annual platform fee, the 2.5% property-management fee plus VAT, the owners' association charge and the 2% exit fee were on no screen.

**After.** One list in three groups (paid once, paid yearly, paid on exit) that adds up on screen to 6,076,000 in total and 108.50 per share. Each row is named the way the contract names it, with its own source. No competitor in the set prints a recurring or exit percentage anywhere, so printing them is a reason to choose this app.

**In the field.** Peer A itemises eleven entry-cost lines, each with its own explanation, and names its own fee in the list. Its recurring charges appear only inside a downloadable spreadsheet, and no exit fee appears in the interface.

## 8. Due diligence

**Before.** The strongest evidence the app had, two independent valuations by valuers licensed with the Saudi Authority for Accredited Valuers, was delivered as dense scanned PDFs the investor was expected to read and reconcile alone.

**After.** A key-facts digest. It shows the two valuations side by side with their dates, values and the per-share figure each implies, the lease as a structured card, and the contract's clauses as cards you can read one at a time. The original documents stay one tap behind, as the audit trail.

## 9. The portfolio

**Before.** An empty wallet illustration, three zeroes and a faint chart line. Nothing told a would-be owner what owning would look like, and the screen was a dead end at the moment the customer was closest to buying.

**After.** Two tabs, both factual. Distributions plots one bar per payment actually deposited, each opening its own statement. Share value plots a range on one riyals-per-share axis: the purchase price, the two valuations as a band, the reference price, and what this investor paid. A note explains that the share price includes property tax and the platform fee, which a valuation of the property doesn't, and that there is no continuous market price for a share. No chart appears before the first payout.

**In the field.** Every peer that draws a portfolio chart draws it rising. Peer C draws one over an empty portfolio, beside a tile reading "growth rate 8.0%". Peer A has no income history at all, only a forward projection.

## 10. Payout statements

**Before.** Money arrived in the wallet as a line item. There was no statement, no per-share working and no export, so an owner couldn't check what they were paid and an accountant couldn't use it.

**After.** Each payout has a statement that shows the whole sum: net rent for the period ÷ 56,000 shares × your shares = your amount, with the deposit date and the deducted fees named. A CSV export writes the same rows in a form spreadsheets can read (ISO dates, ungrouped amounts, and a byte-order mark so Excel opens the Arabic), downloaded or sent to the account's registered email.

**In the field.** Peer A emails a CSV of transactions with four period options. The original app was already ahead on statement content and only behind on getting data out.

## 11. The exit

**Before.** "Early exit · soon", and one sentence saying windows would open according to the regulator's guidance. No price rule, lock-up, fee or settlement time.

**After.** A designed journey. It includes a quarterly window with a countdown, and a reference price equal to the mean of the two valuations per share (102.87 in Lebna's numbers). Offers sit within ±10% of it, after a six-month lock-up from deed registration, and the 2% fee is shown against what the shares cost you. Orders match in parts with each fill logged, the deed share transfers at the Real Estate Registry, and net proceeds reach the wallet within five working days. The second route the contract allows, selling the whole property by owners' vote, is there too.

**In the field.** Peer A shows the finished version of this idea. Its deal page has an exit-options card, and a past-performance screen for the last window that includes the buyers-per-seller ratio, a number that admits the window had more sellers than buyers.

## 12. Questions, and who answers them

**Before.** The answer to "what returns should I expect?" was a paragraph telling the reader to consult the opportunity file, the document they were already struggling to read. Support was a list of three channels with no hours and no stated response time.

**After.** An assistant and a live chat that share one answer function and one rule: they answer only from the documents. Every reply prints its source and links to the screen that proves it. A forecast or an opinion ("will the price rise?", "what do you think?") is refused before any keyword can match. Anything the documents don't cover becomes a ticket with a reference number. Availability is the published hours checked against the real clock, with no "24/7" and no invented response time.

**In the field.** Peer C has an AI assistant in the centre tab and on every deal page, aware of which opportunity you're looking at. It answers freely, which is the risk. Answering only from documents is the difference.

## 13. Teaching the model

**Before.** A product most Saudis have never bought, taught through one explainer card and a FAQ list. There was no path from "I don't understand shares in a building" to "I'm ready to subscribe".

**After.** Five lessons covering the share, the deed, payouts, fees and the exit. Each is a screen built from the deal's own figures, with a recap, a named source and one link onward. The reading time beside each is calculated from that lesson's own text at a published reading rate, and the hub's total is the sum of the parts. Completing a lesson is an action, not a side effect of opening it.

**In the field.** Peer A has lessons with stated runtimes, presented by named staff, inside the wallet where the questions come up.

## 14. The account

**Before.** Seven rows, two of them marked "soon". A language row with nothing behind it. A personal account with four items and no bank account, no verification status, no security section and no route to a statement, for a product whose premise is that the deed is registered in your name.

**After.** The account leads with who you are and how far you're verified, then the bank account payouts land in, then the tools an owner opens: automatic services, the academy, CSV export, gifting, the Hijri-year Zakat statement and notifications. Nothing is marked "soon". A passcode and biometrics lock the app, and a withdrawal or an IBAN change needs a fresh one-time code.

**In the field.** Peer D has biometric and passcode locking, but buried in settings and off by default.

## 15. Coming back

Between one opportunity and the next, an app has two jobs: tell people something true, and give them a reason to bring someone.

**Before.** An empty notification inbox, and a notify button with no rule behind it. Between subscriptions there was no reason to open the app and no record that anything was happening. The referral screen offered "free credit" with no amount, no condition and no date.

**After.** The update feed is built from the record's own state, so it can't announce an event the app hasn't recorded: subscription opened, funding closed with the investor count, transfer, deed registered with its number, each payout with its per-share figure, the manager's report and the valuations. Alerts are standing rules. The referral reward states its terms on the same screen: it's paid when the friend invests at least the minimum and the deal closes, credited to both wallets. A visitor without an account is never asked to invite anyone.

## What the original app already did better

These were the reasons not to start from scratch, and each survives the redesign on the screen where the decision is made:

- **Printed fee percentages.** The only app of the six to show recurring and exit percentages at all.
- **Two independent valuations.** Every peer shows one value or none.
- **Per-share payout arithmetic.** Nobody else shows how a payment was calculated.
- **Ownership at the Real Estate Registry.** The deed is in the investor's name, not the platform's, which answers "what if the platform closes?"
- **Verification deferred to the moment of investing.** It was half-built in the original app. The redesign finishes the idea.
